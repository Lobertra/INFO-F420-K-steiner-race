The race between the two pruning methods

Both algorithms look for the shortest tree connecting the terminals with at most k Steiner points. They examine the same topologies in the same order: every group of 3 vertices (degree-3 Steiner point, Y) and every group of 4 vertices (degree-4 Steiner point, X, at the crossing point of the diagonals). With k ≥ 2, they place one Steiner point per round, then adjust their positions. The new method prunes more degree-4 Steiner points without evaluating their tree, thanks to the Trapezium Theorem. With k = 1, both find the same Steiner point: the new one simply proves faster that it is optimal. At the end, the best tree found is shown on each drawing.

      Cost model and simplifications

      A geometric test (convexity, angle, Rhombus Test, Trapezium Test) costs 1 unit. Evaluating a tree, that is, the minimum spanning tree of the terminals and the Steiner point, costs (n+1)² units (Prim's algorithm). The new method sometimes does more tests, but evaluates fewer trees.

      For k = 1, the search is exact. For k ≥ 2, the Steiner points are added one by one: every round adds the best Steiner point (among the triples and quadruples of terminals and Steiner points already placed), then moves every Steiner point to its exact position for its current neighbours (Theorem 2.4: at 2π/3 for degree 3, at the crossing point of the diagonals for degree 4). The result is very good, but not always optimal.

      This is a simplified version: in the paper, the pruning tests are applied while the branches are merged (generation phase), not on a complete list of topologies.

      Reference: J. Lee, M. Brazil, C. Ras, D. A. Thomas. An improved exact algorithm for the Euclidean k-Steiner tree problem. Computational Geometry: Theory and Applications, 135:102268, 2026.
